Today, let's look at how trees can help cities deal with hot weather. Trees cool streets in two main ways. Their leaves shade pavements and buildings, so those surfaces absorb less sunlight. Trees also release water through their leaves, which can cool the surrounding air.

However, planting a tree does not guarantee an immediate benefit. A young tree may take years to grow a broad canopy. It also needs enough soil, water and room for its roots. A small tree surrounded by dry pavement may struggle to survive and provide very little shade.

The choice of species matters as well. A tree that grows well in a wet climate may need expensive irrigation in a dry city. Underground pipes and cables can also limit where a tree can be planted. Planners therefore need to consider the conditions at each site.

Residents can water young trees and report damage. Councils still need to plan for long-term maintenance. Trees are valuable, but they cannot solve urban heat on their own. Shaded public spaces and buildings designed for hot weather are also important.

Finally, how can a city tell whether its programme is working? Counting newly planted trees is not enough. Officials should check how many trees survive and how much canopy cover develops over time. They can compare neighbourhoods across several summers, especially where streets have little shade. This evidence helps planners decide where future investment would be most useful.
